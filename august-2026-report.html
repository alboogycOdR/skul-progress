Monthly Progress Report

August 2026 — sponsor update

Period covered: 1 – 22 August 2026 · Prepared by SKULL_DEV_TEAM · Published 22 August 2026

August was the month SKUL.CO.ZM stopped being a project being tested and became a service being run. The platform went permanently live on real payment credentials, all three mobile money operators were proven with real money, and the safety net a live service needs — backups, monitoring, alerts — was put in place. Then, on 8 August, we corrected something significant that had gone unnoticed since March: the website had been delivered to learners in the mode normally used while a site is being built, rather than the mode used to publish it. Fixing it made the site substantially lighter for learners on basic phones, and closed two matters that should not have been left standing.

Part one

Going live properly

The platform went permanently live

Until this month, going live had been a series of temporary windows: switch to real payment credentials, test, switch back. On the evening of 4 August the platform moved to real credentials permanently. The business owner began onboarding real learners the following morning.

The changeover was done carefully rather than quickly. A backup was taken immediately beforehand as a clean boundary point, the change was compared against the existing settings before being applied, and afterwards we confirmed that no learner data had shifted during the switch — the counts of learners, subscriptions, payments and curriculum content all matched exactly what they had been before.

All three mobile money operators now proven

On 1 August, MTN and Zamtel were both tested with real money — a genuine purchase, not a simulation. Both worked first time, and both correctly activated the learner's subscription automatically. Combined with Airtel, which was proven in July, all three operators a Zambian learner might use are now confirmed working.

One honest qualification

Zamtel had never been confirmed as working with our payment provider at all before this test. It resolved positively, but it has one clean success behind it and has not been tested against failure cases. Airtel remains the most thoroughly exercised of the three.

Backups now exist, and have been proven to work

This is worth stating plainly, because the previous position was poor: before this month there were no backups of the live system at all, and no schedule to create any.

Now there is a backup taken automatically every night at 3am, kept for a fortnight, with a second copy held away from the main server and verified as identical. More importantly, we did not simply set backups running and assume they work. We performed a restore drill — took a backup, restored it into a separate scratch database, confirmed every record came back correctly, then discarded the scratch copy. The live system was never touched during this.

A backup that has never been restored is a hope, not a safeguard. This one has been restored.

Cost control

We found the cloud spending alert set to a threshold of about $12 a month, which real usage would have breached almost immediately — meaning it would have fired constantly and quickly been ignored. It has been raised to a realistic $200 a month with warnings at 50%, 80% and 100%.

For the sponsor's awareness

This alerts; it does not cap. Nothing automatically stops spending at $200 — it simply tells us we have got there. A hard cap is a separate decision and carries its own risk, since it would take the service offline rather than overspend. We have not taken that decision unilaterally.

Monitoring and alerting

Three independent layers now watch the service:

  1. Uptime monitoring — checks from outside that the site is reachable.
  2. Signup and payment alerts — an email every time a learner registers or a payment succeeds, checked every five minutes. Deliberately built to run completely outside the platform, so that a fault in the alerting cannot affect the service it is watching.
  3. Nightly backups — as above.

Launch announcement page

A temporary announcement page was published to mark go-live. One decision here is worth recording for the sponsor, as it concerns safeguarding.

The page included a video featuring identifiable minors. The video and its still image were deliberately not stored in the project's permanent history, because that history is effectively impossible to erase — the files would persist in every future copy of the project long after the page itself was taken down. Consent had been given for the launch page specifically, which is the narrower thing, so the files live only on the server that serves them. We would rather be careful here than convenient.

Part two

Making the service safe to operate

Written-down knowledge, verified against reality

A one-line change to the website took three failed attempts and two wrong diagnoses. The cause was not the change; it was that our understanding of how the live system was assembled was wrong in a specific way.

The response was to write a reference document in which every single fact was read directly from the live server, not from memory or from project files that might have drifted. This is dull work with no visible output, and it is the reason later work that month went smoothly. A companion document was written covering what to check first when something breaks, so that diagnosis does not depend on who is available.

A real problem with curriculum coverage, found and contained

Our curriculum content covers Grades 8, 9 and 10 only. However, learners could register as Form 4 or Form 5 students.

The consequence was quiet and serious: the tutor matches its answers against the syllabus content for the learner's grade. A Form 4 or Form 5 learner matched no syllabus content whatsoever, and so received answers with no curriculum grounding at all — with nothing on screen to indicate it. That is the opposite of what the platform promises.

Form 4 and Form 5 have been temporarily removed from registration until the content exists to support them. This is a deliberate reduction in what we offer rather than continuing to offer something we cannot yet deliver properly.

Decision point for the sponsor

The platform currently serves three year groups. Extending to Forms 4 and 5 is a content exercise, not a software one, and it has not been scheduled.

Part three — 8 August

The website delivery correction

This was the most substantial single day of the month, and the rest of this report covers it in detail.

What was wrong

Modern websites can be delivered two ways.

The building mode keeps every part of the website separate, so that a developer can change one small thing and see it immediately. Convenient while working — but a visitor's phone must then request hundreds of small pieces individually and assemble them itself.

The published mode compresses and packages everything into a few small files, delivered in one go.

SKUL.CO.ZM had been running in the building mode since March, including for every real learner since go-live. A fair comparison: it is like serving customers from the stockroom rather than the shop floor. Everyone gets what they came for, but it is slower, less tidy, and they can see things that were never meant for them.

How it happened — the honest account

We looked into the history, and it is worth being straightforward.

Nobody decided this. When the project was first set up in March, the website was configured in building mode — the ordinary starting point for any new project. In April, when the site was first placed behind its public web address, that configuration stopped working correctly. It was adjusted so that it would work publicly, rather than replaced with the published mode. The quicker fix was chosen at the time, under pressure to get the testing environment running.

That April change is the moment the problem became real, and it was made while fixing something else entirely. Because the site worked, nothing since forced the question. The preparations for published mode already existed in the project from early on — the final step of switching over was simply never taken.

For our learners specifically — low-end Android phones on slow, often metered connections — the third row is the one that matters. Fewer requests and less data means the site loads faster and costs the learner less.

Two matters closed that should not have been left standing

Learners' passwords were being written into their own browser's log

Four diagnostic messages had been left in the website's code. One of them recorded the full contents of every request the site made — which meant that at sign-in, a learner's password in plain text, and the token that identifies them afterwards, were written into their browser's developer log.

To be precise about the risk, because it should neither be overstated nor waved away: this log is visible only on the learner's own device, to someone who opens the developer tools. It was not transmitted anywhere, and there is no indication it was accessed. It should nonetheless never have been there, and it is now gone.

Internal project files were readable by anyone visiting the site

Because of the building mode, several of the project's own configuration files were fetchable over the web by anyone who knew to ask for them. We verified this directly — they returned real content.

We also verified the limit of the exposure: no passwords, keys or credentials were among them. The website portion of the project holds no such file, and requests for one returned nothing. The exposure was of structure and configuration, not secrets. Both issues are fixed and the files are no longer present in the deployed site at all.

A third matter, visible to visitors. On 6 August a visitor moving between pages was briefly shown a developer error message across the whole screen. It cleared on refresh. This was a symptom of the same underlying cause, and it prompted the investigation that led to all of the above.

A failure that would have gone unnoticed — closed by design

After the correction was deployed, the live site depended on a single line of configuration held only on the server and not stored in the project's records. Had that file ever been restored from the project, the site would have silently reverted to the building mode: no error, no warning, and nothing in our monitoring would have caught it.

Rather than write this down as a caution for someone to remember, we changed the arrangement so that the correct behaviour is now the default. Anyone setting up the project fresh gets the published mode automatically. The only way to get the building mode is to ask for it explicitly, and the file that does so cannot reach a server.

A principle to hold us to

Where a safeguard matters, it should be something that is checked, not something that has to be remembered.

Better error messages for learners

Separately, any learner who mistyped their password was told “Session expired. Please log in again.” — meaningless, since they had never been signed in, and no hint that the password was simply wrong. They are now told the password is incorrect.

Candour

What we found wrong in our own records

Three things this project had written down as true were found to be false, all on the same day.

  • A safety control that our documentation described as being switched on had, in fact, never been switched on.
  • Our documentation described a routine quality check that has never existed at all.
  • A duplicated copy of the project's own instructions had drifted out of date and was contradicting the real one on four points — including which payment provider we use and how many free questions a learner receives.

We are reporting this because the pattern matters more than the individual errors: written assurances decay silently, and nothing tells you when. Any of the three could have produced confidently wrong work.

The response was not to write more documentation. It was to build an automated check that inspects the live system and reports pass or fail against eleven specific conditions — the things we had previously only asserted. It is now run before and after any change to the live service.

Assessment

Where this leaves us

The service is live, monitored, backed up, and has been running without interruption. Payments work across all three operators with real money. The most significant technical debt carried since March has been repaid, and the two matters found alongside it have been closed.

The candid part: several of this month's most valuable pieces of work were corrections of things that should not have been the case — a site running in the wrong mode for five months, no backups on a live service, an alert threshold set so low it would have been ignored, and our own records asserting safeguards that did not exist. We would rather report that plainly than present the month as uninterrupted progress.

Two things now protect against a repeat: the automated check described above, and a working practice of verifying claims against the live system rather than against what is written down.

The largest outstanding risk is not technical. It is that testing currently happens on the same server and database that serves real learners. That is the item we would ask the sponsor to consider funding next.
